The world is 76% online. Two billion people still aren't.

The ITU's 2026 numbers show enormous progress and a stubborn digital divide. Having mobile coverage, a phone and useful internet access are not the same thing.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
WorldThe Offline DivideC·

More than three-quarters of the world is online. That sounds like a success story—and, in part, it is. But the same 2026 estimate from the International Telecommunication Union says around two billion people still do not use the internet. The headline number does not tell the whole story.

The ITU estimated 6.3 billion internet users in 2026, or 76% of the global population, compared with a revised 6.1 billion in 2025. More people are connected. The harder question is whether the people still offline can realistically afford the service, equipment and skills that useful access demands.

The economic divide is hard to ignore. Internet use reaches an estimated 93% of people in high-income countries, but only 32% in low-income countries. A global average hides a gap that can affect education, communication, public services and opportunities to apply for work.

Coverage is not the same as meaningful connection. A community may have a mobile signal but still lack affordable plans, suitable devices or dependable speeds. A shared or unreliable connection might work for messages and fail when someone needs to attend a class or complete a job application.

The ITU also tracks affordability. According to its 2026 assessment, three in ten economies meet neither its fixed-broadband nor mobile-broadband affordability target. Infrastructure can exist on paper without being affordable to the people expected to use it.

Comparing years requires care as well. The ITU revised its 2025 total upward, so an older chart using six billion and a newer one using 6.1 billion may not line up. Statistical revisions are not proof of dishonesty; they are a reminder to check which edition and definitions a number comes from.

Access to the internet increasingly shapes whether someone can participate in everyday life. Celebrating the 76% milestone makes sense. Treating it as the end of the problem does not. The next measure of progress should consider reliability, affordability and the people who remain excluded.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed the ITU's Facts and Figures 2026 report and press release. We did not conduct field reporting or original interviews.
