An earthquake alert can beat the shaking. It cannot predict the quake.

Early-warning systems detect earthquakes after rupture begins and try to deliver a warning before stronger waves arrive, leaving little time near the epicenter.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceSeconds Before ShakingC·

An earthquake warning arriving seconds before shaking can seem like a prediction. It is not. Systems such as the U.S. Geological Survey's ShakeAlert detect an earthquake that has already begun, process sensor signals and send information quickly enough that some people may receive an alert before stronger shaking reaches them.

The advantage comes from physics and telecommunications. The first seismic waves travel through the ground faster than later, more damaging waves; electronic messages can travel faster still. Sensors can therefore identify the beginning of a significant event and issue a message to partner systems.

That window is not guaranteed. People close to the earthquake's origin may feel shaking before an alert arrives, while others farther away may have more notice. The actual warning time depends on location, the quake's characteristics, sensor coverage and how quickly an alert is delivered.

The USGS describes ShakeAlert as a detection and notification system rather than a prediction service. It cannot tell people with certainty which day, hour or location a future earthquake will occur. Advertising it as a device that forecasts earthquakes would misrepresent its purpose.

Alert partners can translate a ShakeAlert message into public notifications or automated actions. Examples include slowing trains and protecting equipment. The underlying goal is to reduce risk during an event already in progress, not to remove the hazard.

Earthquake magnitude and the shaking felt at a specific location also differ. A large quake far away may create a different local risk from a smaller event close by. Alert thresholds and how much shaking is expected matter more to an individual notification than the headline magnitude alone.

The useful takeaway is modest but important: an early-warning system can create a small opportunity for protective action, but it should not be confused with a guarantee or a substitute for building safety and preparedness.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed the USGS ShakeAlert technical overview and agency outreach documents. We did not test an alert system or analyze private seismic data.
