Rising seas have two main engines—and neither is a mystery

Land ice adds water to the ocean, while warmer seawater expands. Local coastlines can experience additional factors.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceThe Rising BaselineC·

Sea level rise is sometimes discussed as though the ocean is simply receiving water from melting ice. That is an important part of the process, but not the whole explanation. Warmer seawater also occupies more volume.

NOAA identifies two main drivers of global sea level rise: the expansion of seawater as it warms and additional water from melting land-based ice. Melting floating sea ice has a different direct effect on sea level than melting glaciers on land.

NASA monitors global sea level with satellites, allowing scientists to track changes across decades. Its measurements show a rising long-term pattern even though the rate can vary from one year to another.

Local sea level is not identical to the global average. Land subsidence, regional ocean conditions and other factors can cause some shorelines to experience a faster or slower relative change.

That is why flooding risk cannot be read from a global graph alone. Infrastructure, tides, storms, land elevation and local geography influence what a particular coastal community faces.

The useful question is both global and local: how much is the ocean rising on average, and what is happening relative to the land where people live? Keeping those measures separate makes coastal planning more credible.

Reporting note: Civora used NOAA and NASA sea-level explanations and measurements. No local coastal model was produced.
