Astronomers can discover a planet by watching a star get slightly dimmer

The transit method finds possible exoplanets in repeating changes in starlight. Confirmation requires ruling out other causes.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceA Dip in StarlightC·

Many exoplanets are too distant and faint to photograph directly. Astronomers instead look for indirect evidence, including tiny reductions in a star's brightness that occur when a planet crosses between the star and the observer.

This is called the transit method. NASA explains that researchers measure a star's light over time and look for patterns that could indicate an orbiting planet. A repeatable dip can reveal an orbital period.

The size of the brightness change can help estimate the planet's size relative to its star. But a light curve is not a photograph of the planet, and it does not automatically reveal whether the surface has oceans or life.

Other effects can imitate a transit. Stellar activity, background objects or observational errors may create confusing signals. Scientists therefore test alternatives and often gather more data before confirming a planet.

Additional methods, including radial-velocity measurements, can help constrain a planet's properties. Combining different types of evidence can turn a promising candidate into a much stronger conclusion.

The extraordinary part of this method is not a single dramatic image. It is the precision required to identify a repeating signal in distant light and then establish that the most likely explanation is a real world orbiting another star.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed NASA's explanations of transit detection and confirmation. This article does not claim a newly discovered planet.
