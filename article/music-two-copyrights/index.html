You made the song. Do you actually own the recording?

One track can involve two separate copyrights. For musicians releasing independently, confusing composition rights with recording rights is an expensive mistake.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
Music & EntertainmentBehind the SongC·

Releasing music is exciting until the ownership questions show up. You wrote the melody, someone else recorded the vocals, another person produced the track—and now everyone calls it 'our song.' That phrase is not a copyright agreement.

U.S. law generally treats a musical composition and a sound recording as separate copyrighted works. The composition covers the underlying music and lyrics. The recording protects a particular captured performance of those elements. One finished track can therefore involve different rights holders.

Picture a songwriter who writes a piece and another artist who records a new version. The recording artist may own rights to that new recording without owning the composition itself. The original songwriter's rights do not disappear just because someone else released a polished version.

This becomes important the moment music moves beyond private listening. A license for a particular recording is not automatically permission to use its composition, and permission to perform or record a composition does not give someone the right to copy another artist's master. Covers, sampling and video synchronization can raise different questions.

Copyright protection generally begins when original expression is fixed, such as in a written score or audio file. Registration is not always necessary for protection to arise, though it can matter substantially when enforcing rights. The U.S. Copyright Office provides separate registration guidance for musical works and sound recordings.

Credits do not settle ownership by themselves. A musician might perform without writing, and a producer might contribute creatively without owning the songwriting. Actual contributions, contracts and applicable law determine the result; an informal group chat is a poor substitute for a clear agreement.

Before a release, identify who wrote the music and lyrics, who owns the master recording, what permissions have been granted, and how revenue and credits will be handled. Keep those answers in writing. Sorting it out before distribution is easier than arguing over a popular track afterward.

Reporting note: This is a general explanation drawn from U.S. Copyright Office guidance, not legal advice about a specific contract or release.
