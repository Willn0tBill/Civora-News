The ocean can become more acidic without turning into acid.

Extra atmospheric carbon dioxide changes seawater chemistry and can affect organisms that build shells and skeletons.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceA Chemistry Change at SeaC·

Scientists say the ocean is acidifying, and a misleading response asks why seawater still has a pH above seven. The wording describes a direction of change: seawater can become less alkaline without becoming acidic in the everyday sense.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration explains that seawater absorbs some atmospheric carbon dioxide. The resulting chemistry alters the balance of dissolved carbon species, lowering pH and affecting the availability of carbonate ions.

Many shell-building organisms depend on carbonate chemistry. Changes can complicate the formation or maintenance of calcium-carbonate shells and skeletons, although effects vary by species, life stage and local conditions.

Ocean acidification is related to climate change but is not the same measurement as water temperature. A warming ocean and changing seawater chemistry can interact while presenting distinct biological stresses.

Regional variability matters. Currents, upwelling, biological activity and freshwater inputs influence local chemistry. A global trend should not be represented as a uniform daily reading in every bay.

Clear reporting distinguishes the pH scale, carbonate saturation and observed biological effects. A small numerical pH shift can represent an important chemical change because the scale is logarithmic.
