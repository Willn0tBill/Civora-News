The Fed held rates steady. Two officials wanted them higher.

The October 28 Federal Reserve vote kept its benchmark rate target at 3.75%–4.00%, but two dissents exposed a disagreement about inflation risk.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Markets & EconomyTwo Votes Against the HoldC·

A central bank decision can look unanimous when reduced to one sentence. The Federal Reserve's October 28, 2026 decision was not. The Federal Open Market Committee maintained its target range for the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00%, with two members voting for a quarter-point increase instead.

The Fed said the decision passed 10–2. Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack preferred to raise the target range by 0.25 percentage point. Their opposition matters because the disagreement was about tightening policy further, not lowering rates.

The committee described economic activity as expanding strongly, with resilient domestic spending, robust investment and job gains broadly keeping pace with the workforce. It also said inflation remained elevated. The statement presents a familiar tension: supporting employment while trying to return inflation to the institution's 2% goal.

The federal funds rate is an overnight borrowing target between banks. It influences broader financing conditions, but it does not directly set the interest rate on a particular mortgage, student loan or credit card. Individual lenders also price risk, funding costs and loan terms.

A rate hold is an action, not the absence of one. By leaving the target unchanged, officials preserve the existing stance while evaluating new data. A dissenting member can agree with the broad objective of price stability and still disagree about how much restraint is needed now.

The public statement tells readers how members voted; it does not provide a full transcript of each participant's private reasoning. The formal meeting minutes and subsequent remarks can provide additional context. Nor does an October vote by itself guarantee what policymakers will decide at their next meeting.

The meaningful conclusion is narrower than 'the Fed declared victory' or 'the Fed panicked.' Policymakers maintained the rate range, identified ongoing inflation concerns and demonstrated an internal split over whether those risks justified raising rates. Those are the facts on the record.

Reporting note: Civora analyzed the Federal Reserve's October 28 public decision and its FOMC policy documentation. We did not attend the meeting or interview committee members.
