Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions for a reason.

A previously issued bond's fixed payments become more or less attractive when market rates change, altering its price.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe Bond SeesawC·

Interest rates rise, and a headline says bond prices fell. For readers who think of bonds as loans that pay interest, the inverse relationship can sound backwards. The missing piece is the price people will pay for an existing stream of promised payments.

A conventional fixed-rate bond promises specified coupon payments and principal under its terms. If newly issued comparable bonds offer higher yields, an existing lower-coupon bond generally becomes less attractive at its old price.

Its market price can decline until the expected return to a buyer better matches available alternatives. When yields fall, the reverse relationship typically pushes the price of a fixed-rate bond upward, all else equal.

The effect is not identical for every security. Maturity, coupon, credit risk, options and other characteristics influence sensitivity. A bond that may default has different considerations from a Treasury security backed by the federal government.

Holding a bond to maturity can change which price movements matter to the investor, but it does not remove credit, inflation or opportunity costs. Selling before maturity exposes the owner to market value at that moment.

The careful way to read a bond headline is to distinguish coupon rate, current price and yield to maturity. All three describe the same instrument from different angles; none is a universal promise of profit.
