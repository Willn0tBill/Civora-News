A $49.75 million rental settlement puts hidden housing fees under scrutiny

The FTC and four states accuse Progress Residential and its owner of misleading renters. A proposed settlement sets out payments and pricing changes; the case was pending when announced.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe Cost of RentingC·

A rental listing gives you one monthly price. Then come required fees, charges at move-in, and deductions when you leave. If the total cost is not clear upfront, comparing homes fairly becomes much harder. That is the issue at the center of a major federal housing enforcement case.

On November 6, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission and four states announced a proposed $49.75 million settlement with Progress Residential and its owner, Pretium Partners, over alleged deceptive rental-pricing and related practices. The FTC describes the matter as its largest enforcement case against a landlord to date.

According to the complaint, Progress advertised monthly rents without adequately disclosing mandatory recurring charges. Regulators also alleged problems involving the condition of homes at move-in, security-deposit deductions, customer service and contract provisions that restricted negative reviews.

Progress manages more than 90,000 single-family rental homes across 17 states, according to the FTC. The agency says some renters discovered charges only after signing a lease or moving in, when switching homes might no longer be a realistic option.

The proposed settlement calls for $48.5 million to the FTC, intended for consumer redress, and another $1.25 million to Arizona. It also requires clearer disclosure of the true total monthly rental price and restrictions on misleading claims about property inspections, deposits and customer service.

There is a legal distinction worth keeping front and center: these are allegations in an enforcement complaint and terms of a proposed court order. The FTC's case page identified the matter as pending when the November 6 announcement was published. A proposed order is not the same as a court finding after a trial.

The FTC release says the complaint alleges that most of the conduct occurred under prior management and that new management has taken steps to address problems. That context does not eliminate the regulators' allegations, but it belongs in a complete account.

For renters, the practical issue is transparency. A price comparison should include mandatory monthly fees, not just the number most prominently displayed. Move-in conditions and security-deposit terms are also part of the cost of renting, even when a listing makes them harder to see.

A settlement may change company practices, but it does not instantly resolve every renter's experience. The next things to verify are court approval, the final order and how any consumer-redress process is administered.
