A busy shop can still be below break-even.

Sales volume must cover both variable and fixed costs; foot traffic alone does not reveal whether a business is sustainable.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe Break-Even LineC·

A cafe serves a steady line of customers and looks successful from the sidewalk. But activity is not profitability. The business still has to cover ingredients, rent, wages, utilities and other costs before there is an operating surplus.

Break-even analysis asks how much must be sold for relevant revenue to cover relevant costs. The answer depends on what each sale contributes after variable costs and how much the operation must pay even when there are few customers.

Consider two products with the same sticker price but different direct costs. Selling more of the high-cost product may produce less contribution toward rent than selling fewer of a lower-cost product. Revenue alone obscures the mix.

Fixed costs also change in practice. A business may need another employee, larger equipment or more space after expanding. A break-even calculation built for one level of activity can become outdated.

The Small Business Administration emphasizes planning startup expenses and financial management. Those exercises are related but not identical: a business can have a favorable projected margin and still face a short-term cash shortage.

A strong business story separates traffic, average sale, contribution margin, recurring expenses and cash timing. A crowded storefront is evidence of customers, not by itself proof of financial health.
