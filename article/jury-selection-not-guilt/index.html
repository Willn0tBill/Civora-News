A jury summons is an invitation to a process—not a seat on a trial.

Federal courts select potential jurors from pools and question candidates before a final jury hears evidence.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
U.S.Inside Jury SelectionC·

A jury summons arriving in the mail can feel like a decision has already been made about someone's role. In federal court, it is the start of a selection process, not proof the person will sit on a particular trial.

The federal judiciary says district courts randomly draw prospective jurors from lists covering their communities, including voter records and sometimes other sources. Potential jurors may complete a qualification questionnaire so courts can assess statutory requirements and eligibility.

When a trial needs a jury, the court brings in a group of qualified people. The judge and lawyers can ask questions about whether candidates can consider the case fairly. This stage, known as voir dire, may result in excusals or permitted challenges.

Jury selection is not a finding about the defendant or the plaintiff. The final jury hears evidence under rules administered by the judge. In a trial, the jury's job is to determine facts and follow legal instructions, not to vote on popular opinion.

Federal courts distinguish trial juries from grand juries, which serve a different function in criminal charging procedures. Summonses, qualifications and service rules can also differ in state systems, so a federal example should not be presented as universal.

A useful explanation of a high-profile trial should identify which stage the case has reached, how many people are being considered and whether opening statements or evidence have begun. Picking a jury is preparation for a trial, not its verdict.
