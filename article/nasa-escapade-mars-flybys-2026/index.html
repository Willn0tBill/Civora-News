NASA's two Mars probes just pulled off an unusual double Earth flyby

ESCAPADE used gravity and engine burns to send two spacecraft toward Mars. The maneuver worked; the real scientific test is still ahead.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceThe Long Way to MarsC·

NASA has sent two small spacecraft toward Mars using a route that sounds backward: launch, spend months near Earth, return for a close flyby, and only then head for the Red Planet. On November 9, the agency confirmed that both ESCAPADE spacecraft completed the crucial maneuvers.

ESCAPADE stands for Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers. The twin spacecraft combined Earth's gravity with a sustained engine firing, a technique NASA describes as its first powered Earth flyby used to send spacecraft toward Mars. Both maneuvers were successful, according to the agency.

The first spacecraft made its close approach on November 7, about 1,000 miles above Earth, and performed an engine burn lasting roughly nine minutes. The second followed on November 9, approaching at about 650 miles and conducting its own burn. Those details matter because a flyby is not the same as arriving at Mars; each probe needed the right speed and direction for the next leg.

The mission launched from Florida in November 2025, when Earth and Mars were not positioned for a conventional direct trip. Instead of waiting for a more favorable departure window, the spacecraft used a looping trajectory near the Earth–Sun L2 region before returning for their November slingshots.

NASA now targets September 2027 for arrival at Mars, with orbital insertion burns planned for September 7 and 9. Arrival is not guaranteed simply because the flybys succeeded. The probes still face months of travel and trajectory corrections.

The scientific goal is to investigate how the solar wind interacts with Mars' magnetic environment and contributes to atmospheric loss. Two spacecraft making coordinated measurements should help researchers separate changes over time from differences between locations.

That scientific work is scheduled to begin after the spacecraft enter Mars orbit and adjust their formation. NASA expects the first coordinated science phase in 2028. Until then, this week's announcement is a navigation milestone rather than a new discovery about Mars.

The larger significance is flexibility. If this route works through arrival and operations, it could give some future missions more options about when and how to depart. But one successful pair of flybys does not mean every mission can abandon the usual launch windows.
