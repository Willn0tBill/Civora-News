GPS works because satellites carry clocks, not just maps.

Positioning systems estimate distance using timing signals, so precise clocks and corrections are fundamental to navigation.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-11
ScienceGPS works because satellites carry clocks, not just mapsC·

A phone displays a blue dot on a road map, making navigation look like a purely visual task. Underneath, satellite positioning depends heavily on time.

Global Positioning System satellites transmit signals containing timing and orbit information. A receiver compares signals from multiple satellites to estimate its position and clock offset.

Because signals travel at the speed of light, small timing errors can become meaningful distance errors. Satellite clocks, ground monitoring, orbital models and mathematical corrections are essential.

GPS also faces limitations in tunnels, dense city streets and locations where buildings or terrain block or reflect signals. A map application's confidence depends on more than the satellites alone.

The takeaway is that a location dot represents a calculation with uncertainty, not a tiny camera watching the user from space. Precision comes from physics and infrastructure.
