A 50% markup is not a 50% profit margin.

Markup and gross margin use different denominators, and confusing them can seriously distort a business's pricing claims.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe Pricing MathC·

A shop buys an item for $10 and sells it for $15. The owner might describe a 50% markup, but the gross margin is roughly one-third of the selling price. The difference is not accounting jargon; it changes how profitability is understood.

Markup divides the increase over purchase cost by the cost. Gross margin compares gross profit with revenue. In the example, the $5 gross profit is half of the $10 cost but only one-third of the $15 sale.

Cost of goods sold depends on what the business sells and applicable accounting rules. Inventory purchases, production costs and direct expenses can influence the calculation. Different kinds of businesses classify costs in different ways.

Gross margin also is not net income. Rent, insurance, payroll expenses not included in cost of goods sold, debt costs and taxes can consume what remains after the product is sold.

Pricing based on the wrong denominator can make a business overestimate how much cash is available for other obligations. Equally, comparing margins from unlike industries without context is misleading.

A credible business report gives the revenue, relevant cost definition and the actual formula being used. 'We marked it up 50%' tells a different story from 'We kept half the sale as gross profit.'
