The ocean stores most of Earth's excess heat. Surface weather only shows a fraction.

Ocean heat content measures warming through depth, helping scientists track an energy imbalance that cannot be understood from air temperature alone.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Earth & OceansHeat Below SurfaceC·

People experience climate change through the air around them, but a major part of Earth's heat story is underwater. The ocean absorbs most of the excess heat accumulating in the climate system.

NOAA's ocean heat content research examines how much thermal energy is stored across different depths. That measure is not the same as the temperature of the water at the beach on a particular afternoon.

Heat can move between the ocean surface and deeper layers through currents and mixing. Researchers use observations from instruments and other systems to estimate changes through time rather than relying only on what happens at the surface.

Ocean warming matters for sea level because water expands as it heats. It also influences marine conditions and the exchange of heat with the atmosphere, though specific local consequences require more detailed study.

Short-term changes in surface temperature can be influenced by weather and ocean circulation. A temporary cool patch does not mean the global ocean stopped accumulating energy.

An ocean heat headline should specify the region, depth, time period and units. Those details help distinguish a meaningful long-term measurement from a snapshot of changing surface conditions.

Reporting note: Civora used NOAA ocean heat research and NASA climate indicators. No original ocean temperature measurements were collected.
