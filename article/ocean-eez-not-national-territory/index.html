A country's ocean economic zone isn't the same as its land border.

Exclusive economic zones provide defined resource rights without making an entire surrounding sea equivalent to sovereign territory.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Diplomacy & International LawWhose Waters?C·

On a map, a broad band of ocean around a coastline may be shaded the same color as the country. That can suggest the sea is national territory in precisely the same way a city or mountain is. Under the law of the sea, the reality is more complicated.

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea provides for an exclusive economic zone, generally extending up to 200 nautical miles from baselines under the treaty's rules. In that zone, coastal states have certain sovereign rights for exploring and managing natural resources.

Those economic rights are not identical to full territorial sovereignty. Other states retain freedoms and lawful uses, including navigation, subject to the convention's terms. Territorial seas, continental shelves and exclusive economic zones are different legal concepts.

Where coastlines sit close together, states may have overlapping claims. The legal question cannot be solved simply by extending two 200-mile lines until they collide; delimitation and applicable international law matter.

Fishing, energy development and scientific research can therefore produce disputes that involve different rights in the same waters. A vessel's presence does not by itself answer whether an unlawful resource operation took place.

The careful way to read an ocean map is to check the boundary type, the treaty basis, the activity and whether the area is disputed. Shading is a visual aid, not a substitute for a legal explanation.
