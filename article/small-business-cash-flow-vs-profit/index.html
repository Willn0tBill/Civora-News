A profitable business can still run out of cash

Revenue, accounting profit and the money available to pay bills are related but separate measures of business health.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Small Business & CommerceCash Is TimingC·

A business can record a successful month on paper and still struggle to pay a supplier on Friday. The problem is timing. Profit measures income against expenses under accounting rules; cash flow tracks money entering and leaving the business.

Consider a company that completes a large project but waits weeks for the customer to pay. It may recognize revenue before the money arrives. Payroll, rent and equipment costs, however, can still come due during that waiting period.

The Small Business Administration emphasizes bookkeeping, financial planning and separating costs when managing a company. Cash-flow projections help owners anticipate upcoming shortages rather than waiting until a payment is missed.

Profit margins matter, but they do not tell the whole story of working capital. Inventory, payment terms and debt obligations can temporarily tie up cash even when the underlying business is viable.

A useful cash forecast needs realistic collection dates and recurring expenses. Optimistic sales figures do little good if customers pay later than expected or a major bill arrives first.

The lesson is not to ignore profitability. It is to measure profitability alongside liquidity. A company needs both a business model that creates value and enough available cash to keep operating while that value is collected.
