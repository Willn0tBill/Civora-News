A '100-year flood' can happen again next year.

The phrase describes a statistical annual probability, not an appointment spaced exactly a century apart.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceThe Flood ProbabilityC·

A river floods during what officials call a '100-year flood.' People understandably assume they are safe from another one for the next century. That is not what the term means. It is a description of statistical probability, not a calendar schedule.

The U.S. Geological Survey explains that a 100-year flood corresponds to a 1% chance of a flood of that size or larger occurring in a given year under the model and conditions being used. Hydrologists increasingly use the phrase '1-percent annual exceedance probability' because it states the meaning more directly.

The probability does not reset as a countdown after a flood. A statistically rare event can happen in consecutive years, just as a low-probability outcome can occur twice in a row. An annual probability is not a promise of a particular waiting period.

These estimates use historical streamflow observations, statistical models and assumptions about the system being studied. A floodplain map is therefore a risk estimate with a method and data behind it, not a perfectly precise boundary between danger and safety.

Changing conditions can complicate older estimates. Land development changes how water moves through a watershed, and climate shifts can change precipitation and flood risks. Historical frequency estimates may need updating when the underlying environment is no longer comparable.

Different words describe different events. A '100-year storm' concerns a precipitation threshold, while a '100-year flood' concerns a flood level or flow. A storm does not always produce the same flood everywhere; soil saturation, terrain and river conditions also matter.

For a homeowner, planner or reader, the better question is which flood probability a local assessment models, when the map was updated and what uncertainty remains. 'One percent a year' is both more accurate and more useful than assuming the danger will not return for 100 years.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed USGS explanations of recurrence intervals and annual exceedance probability. We did not calculate flood probabilities for any particular community.
