Inflation can fall while your grocery bill stays high

Slower inflation means prices are rising more slowly, not that every price is returning to an earlier level.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe Price DifferenceC·

The latest inflation rate declines, and people ask why shopping still feels expensive. The confusion comes from mixing up two different things: the level of prices and the speed at which those prices change.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures the Consumer Price Index using a representative basket of goods and services. Inflation usually describes the percentage change in that index over a chosen period. A lower positive rate means slower price growth, not necessarily a drop in the overall index.

If a product rises from $100 to $110 and then rises another 2%, the new price is $112.20. Inflation slowed compared with the earlier increase, but the product became more expensive again. That simple distinction explains much of the frustration behind inflation headlines.

Households also buy different combinations of products. Rent, food, medical costs and transportation can move differently, so someone's experience may not match the average headline measure.

Economists sometimes distinguish disinflation, a slowdown in the inflation rate, from deflation, a broad decline in the price level. One is not automatically evidence of the other, and neither reveals how wages changed.

The useful question after an inflation report is what the index measured, how fast it changed and which categories drove the result. Affordability also depends on income. A falling inflation rate alone does not settle whether families are better off.

Reporting note: Civora used BLS Consumer Price Index methods and purchasing-power explanations. This is not a forecast of upcoming prices.
