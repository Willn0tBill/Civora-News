Public Wi-Fi is not automatically unsafe. It isn't automatically private, either.

Encryption protects much routine browsing, but impostor networks, device configuration and sensitive account actions still deserve care.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Security & PrivacyA Network You Don't OwnC·

A laptop connects to the wireless network at a cafe. The connection works, and websites open normally. That does not tell the user who controls the network or what other devices may be connected.

The Federal Trade Commission explains that modern encrypted connections protect much information sent to websites, making public Wi-Fi use less risky than it once was for properly configured browsers. HTTPS remains important, particularly for sensitive transactions.

An attacker may still create a network name that resembles a legitimate venue's service or present a deceptive sign-in page. A password on the Wi-Fi network also does not automatically mean traffic is protected end to end against every participant.

Operating-system updates, secure websites and attention to unusual prompts matter on any network. A weakly secured device can be vulnerable at home as well as in public. The fact that a network is free is not itself proof of an attack.

Public Wi-Fi also raises privacy questions. A network operator may see connection details even when page contents are encrypted; the amount depends on protocols and the network setup. Avoiding exaggerated claims makes better security guidance.

The useful approach is to confirm the network with the venue, pay attention to browser security indicators and be cautious with unexpected login requests. A network's convenience should not be mistaken for a complete privacy guarantee.
