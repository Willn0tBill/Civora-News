Earth after dark looks like a map of lights. It's not a map of wealth.

A NASA astronaut's nighttime photograph reveals the shape of cities from orbit. It also shows how easy it is to read too much into a striking image.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
U.S.Cities After DarkC·

From the International Space Station, the eastern United States looks like a network of glowing cities. The photograph is stunning. It is also easy to misuse. Bright lights are evidence of visible illumination at one moment—not a direct ranking of where people live best.

NASA published the view on September 28, 2026 and credited astronaut Jessica Meir with taking it on September 8. The station was about 259 miles above Earth. The New York City region appears near the center, with other population centers forming patterns across the night landscape.

Urban lights reveal highways and developed areas in ways that daytime photographs sometimes do not. But camera exposure, viewing angle, clouds and atmospheric scattering influence what appears bright. Two places with different lighting practices may look dramatically different without telling us much about living standards.

Researchers can use calibrated nighttime observations to study infrastructure and changes in artificial light over time. That requires comparable measurements and a clear method. One eye-catching photo cannot establish an economic trend, measure household income or prove a community lacks electricity.

Another NASA Earth Observatory image published in early November shows the American Midwest at night, including lights around Chicago and Detroit. NASA notes that image processing included cropping and contrast adjustments. Those choices are normal in presenting imagery, but they matter when someone tries to compare brightness between pictures.

Industrial facilities may produce powerful lights even when relatively few people live nearby. Meanwhile, a darker area may be less densely settled or simply use less outdoor lighting. The difference is a warning against turning an image into a statistic it was never designed to provide.

Nighttime views also raise legitimate questions about artificial light and its effects. Those questions need environmental measurements and research, not guesses drawn from the size of a glow on a screen.

The photograph succeeds as a record of what an astronaut saw and as an invitation to investigate. Its limits are just as important as its beauty. Good visual journalism tells readers both what an image shows and what it cannot prove.
