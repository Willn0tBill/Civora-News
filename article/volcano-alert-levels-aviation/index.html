A volcano can threaten aircraft without burying a nearby town in lava.

USGS uses ground hazard levels and aviation color codes to communicate different dimensions of volcanic activity.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceTwo Volcano AlertsC·

A volcano is restless and an aviation alert changes color. That does not automatically mean people on the ground will face the same hazard or that an eruption has already reached a populated area.

The U.S. Geological Survey describes volcano alert levels and aviation color codes as complementary systems. Ground-oriented status helps communicate volcanic activity and related hazards, while aviation codes emphasize the risk of volcanic ash to aircraft.

Ash can affect aircraft engines and visibility far from the volcano. A cloud carried by winds may create an aviation problem even where communities experience little direct effect from the eruption.

An increased alert level is based on observations and interpretations that can change. Scientists monitor earthquakes, deformation, gas and other signs, but conditions can evolve unexpectedly. An alert is a risk communication tool rather than an exact timetable.

Ground hazards also vary by volcano. Lava, ashfall, mudflows and other processes depend on topography and eruption style. A single dramatic photograph should not be used to imply identical impacts everywhere.

A clear report names the alert system, current code or level, time of the observation and official instructions. The story is about the specific hazard being communicated, not just a color.
