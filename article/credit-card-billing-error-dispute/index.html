Banking & Consumer Money / EXPLAINER

A wrong credit-card charge has a clock attached to it.

Federal billing-error protections make timing and a written dispute important, even when a cardholder has already called the bank.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Banking & Consumer MoneyDisputing a ChargeC·

A charge on a statement does not look negotiable when it appears alongside a payment deadline. But the federal billing-error process gives credit-card users a way to dispute certain errors. The important catch is that waiting too long can weaken the protections a consumer might otherwise have.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau advises cardholders to contact their card company promptly and, to preserve rights under the federal billing-error process, send a written notice within 60 calendar days after the disputed charge first appeared on the statement. A telephone conversation by itself is not a reliable substitute for that written step.

The CFPB says the card issuer generally must acknowledge the notice within 30 days unless it has already resolved the dispute. The company then follows the applicable investigation and resolution process. Keep copies of correspondence and the dates of any calls; a dispute without a clear record becomes harder to track.

Not every disagreement fits the same legal category. An unauthorized transaction, an incorrect amount, and a purchase that arrived damaged can involve different rules or card-network procedures. The CFPB specifically notes that defective goods may require a different dispute route. A general 'chargeback' button is not a promise that every purchase will be refunded.

Someone can still raise a dispute after paying the charge, but the CFPB cautions that a refund may not come until the company determines the consumer is correct. If the card issuer rejects the claim, it must provide its explanation in writing under the applicable billing-error procedure.

This process also differs from credit reporting. A mistake on a card statement is about a billing transaction; an inaccurate item on a credit report concerns what credit bureaus hold about a person's history. Both deserve attention, but they are not the same complaint or deadline.

The practical lesson is to read statements rather than treating them as background noise. Identify the specific entry, save receipts, contact the issuer through its official channels and follow the written-notice requirement when it applies. These are general consumer-information steps, not legal advice for a particular dispute.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed federal consumer guidance. We did not examine an individual's bank account, evaluate a specific legal claim or interview cardholders.
