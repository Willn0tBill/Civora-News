Student records are protected, but 'FERPA' is not a universal secrecy rule.

The federal student privacy law gives certain rights over education records, with defined exceptions and limits.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Courts & Civil RightsWho Can See the File?C·

A school official says a record cannot be shared because of FERPA. That might be correct, but the label alone is not a complete legal explanation. The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act protects eligible students and parents in defined ways, not every piece of information that happens to involve a school.

The Department of Education explains that FERPA generally gives parents rights relating to their children's education records; these rights transfer to a student when they become an eligible student, typically at age 18 or upon attendance at a postsecondary institution.

Among the rights are access to education records, an opportunity to seek amendment and controls on certain disclosures of personally identifiable information. But FERPA contains exceptions allowing some disclosures without prior consent, including under conditions specified in the regulations.

Records maintained by a school can raise different questions from personal observations or information not maintained as an education record. Some records fall under specific exclusions. A journalist should not assume any reference to a student is an education record, or that an exception applies without examining the facts.

FERPA also should not be confused with a general right to keep an entire school board meeting private. Public meeting requirements, other privacy laws and the sensitivity of information each involve separate analysis.

For families, the practical first question is which records the school maintains and what access or correction process it provides. For reporters, it is what specific protection or exception is being cited. 'Privacy' matters most when the rule is accurately explained.
