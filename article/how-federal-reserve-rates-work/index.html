The Fed changed rates. Why didn't your mortgage follow?

The Federal Reserve influences borrowing, but it doesn't directly price every loan. Different rates move for different reasons—and not on the same schedule.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
BusinessThe Price of MoneyC·

The Federal Reserve announces an interest-rate decision, and the immediate question is predictable: will mortgages, car loans and credit cards get cheaper? Sometimes borrowing costs move quickly. Sometimes they barely move. Treating every interest rate as if it is controlled by one switch misses how the system works.

The Federal Open Market Committee sets a target range for the federal funds rate, which relates to overnight lending between banks. That rate helps shape broader financial conditions, but the Fed does not directly write the offers consumers see from lenders.

Short-term rates and some variable-rate products can respond to changes in monetary policy. Mortgage rates also reflect longer-term bond yields, inflation expectations, investor demand and assumptions about where future policy is headed. Those forces do not wait politely for the Fed's announcement.

Markets often anticipate a decision. If investors already expected a rate cut, its impact may be reflected in prices before the official statement. It is entirely possible for a Fed move and a mortgage quote to appear disconnected on the same day without either one being a mistake.

The Fed tries to influence inflation and employment through financial conditions. Higher borrowing costs can cool demand and discourage investment; lower costs can support spending. Neither path is instant, and neither guarantees the same outcome for every household or business.

Energy prices, supply problems, confidence and global financial conditions can complicate the result. Monetary policy is powerful, but it is not the only force acting on the economy—and it cannot simply order every price to change.

So read a rate headline in two steps. First, identify what the Fed actually changed. Then check the specific product you're considering and the benchmark that affects it. The headline matters; your loan agreement and the wider market matter too.
