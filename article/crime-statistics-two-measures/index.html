Two official crime statistics can disagree without either being fake

Police-reported incidents and victimization surveys measure different things. Comparing them without methods can turn numbers into misinformation.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
U.S.Reading Crime DataC·

Two crime reports can give different numbers for the same country and year. That does not automatically mean someone falsified a statistic. Often the reports are measuring different things—and the distinction is essential before drawing a political conclusion.

The FBI's crime reporting program collects information from participating law-enforcement agencies. The Bureau of Justice Statistics also conducts the National Crime Victimization Survey, which asks people about some crimes whether or not they were reported to police.

Those datasets have different coverage. Police data can include categories that the household survey does not, while the survey can capture victimizations that never reached police records. Neither instrument is a perfect count of all harm.

Method changes matter, too. The Justice Department documented a redesigned victimization survey in 2025, meaning certain new estimates should not simply be spliced into older series as though nothing changed. Comparing incompatible numbers creates false trends.

Agency participation, definitions and reporting practices are equally important. A chart may legitimately describe reported offenses in a specific dataset, but it cannot automatically answer how safe every neighborhood feels or what every victim experienced.

A responsible crime headline should name the source, categories, geography, period and whether the figures are reported incidents or survey estimates. Those details are not footnotes to the story. They determine what the number means.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed FBI and Bureau of Justice Statistics methodology documents and 2026 reports. No independent crime data analysis was conducted.
