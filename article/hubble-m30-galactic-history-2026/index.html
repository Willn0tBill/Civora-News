Hubble's new portrait of M30 offers clues to the Milky Way's ancient past

A newly released image of the star cluster Messier 30 combines years of Hubble observations—and points to unresolved questions about where some of the galaxy's oldest stars formed.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceAncient Star CitiesC·

NASA and the European Space Agency released a new view of Messier 30 on November 5, 2026. The dazzling cluster is not a newly discovered object. What is new is the portrait, assembled from Hubble Space Telescope observations that span ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared wavelengths. The image provides a way to examine stellar populations in a cluster astronomers have studied for decades.

Messier 30, or M30, lies about 27,500 light-years from Earth in the constellation Capricornus. It is a globular cluster: a densely packed, gravitationally bound group of stars. Globular clusters can hold tens of thousands to millions of stars, and many are old enough to preserve evidence about the early history of the galaxies they orbit.

That age makes M30 useful for reconstructing how the Milky Way assembled. Researchers can study a cluster's stellar chemistry, motion and distribution, then compare those findings with patterns in other clusters. Those clues help investigate whether a group formed within our galaxy or arrived through its interactions with other stellar systems.

NASA and ESA say M30 and several other globular clusters orbit opposite to the Milky Way's overall spin. That unusual motion is consistent with the possibility that some clusters formed elsewhere and were later captured. It is a clue, not definitive proof of any single origin story: astronomers must assess multiple measurements and models.

The latest image draws on three Hubble observing programs. One used the Advanced Camera for Surveys to examine numerous Milky Way clusters in red and near-infrared light. Another used Wide Field Camera 3 and bluer filters to help study different stellar populations. A later program revisited clusters to measure how stars had moved over roughly 15 years.

This is why a space telescope's longevity matters. A second image can be attractive, but measurements years apart can become a record of motion. Combined with observations at different wavelengths, the repeated visits reveal properties a single exposure cannot capture.

The release should not be mistaken for a newly observed collision or a discovery that M30 definitely came from another galaxy. The strongest claim is more measured: the new composite illustrates how long-running observation programs allow scientists to investigate the histories of ancient star clusters.
