Hubble's newest look at M30 is also a look into the Milky Way's past

A new composite image of an old star cluster does more than look impressive: observations across decades help astronomers test where its stars came from.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceAncient Star CitiesC·

An image of a crowded star cluster can look like another spectacular space photograph. NASA and the European Space Agency's new view of Messier 30, released November 5, 2026, has a more interesting story. It brings together Hubble observations that help scientists study some of our galaxy's oldest stellar populations.

Messier 30, commonly called M30, is about 27,500 light-years away in Capricornus. It is a globular cluster, a dense collection of gravitationally bound stars. Clusters like this can preserve clues about conditions and events from the early history of the galaxies around them.

The image combines information collected at ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared wavelengths. Using different parts of the spectrum can make stellar populations easier to distinguish. Revisiting the same cluster years later can also reveal how individual stars move.

NASA and ESA note that M30 and several other globular clusters orbit in a direction opposite the Milky Way's overall rotation. That motion is consistent with the possibility that some clusters came from other stellar systems that merged with our galaxy. It is not a finished origin story, and the observation alone cannot prove one.

Three Hubble programs contributed to the composite. Some observations used the Advanced Camera for Surveys; others relied on Wide Field Camera 3. A later observing effort returned to selected clusters to measure star motions over roughly 15 years.

This is an underrated strength of a long-lived observatory. A new image is not just a better poster for a telescope. It can provide another point in a time series, allowing researchers to test hypotheses that were impossible with one set of observations.

The value of the release is therefore not a claim that astronomers just discovered M30 or watched an ancient galactic collision. The value is the evidence collected across years—and the questions it makes possible to investigate.
