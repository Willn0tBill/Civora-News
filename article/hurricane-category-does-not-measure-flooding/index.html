A hurricane's category does not measure every danger it brings.

Wind ratings are important, but storm surge, rainfall flooding and local exposure may create serious risk outside the highest wind zones.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-11
Climate & WeatherA hurricane's category does not measure every danger it brinC·

A storm weakens from one hurricane category to another, and a headline suggests the danger has faded. The wind classification has changed, but flooding risk may remain severe.

The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale categorizes hurricanes using maximum sustained wind speed. It does not directly include rainfall totals, storm surge or tornado risk.

Coastal water levels depend on storm size, direction, local geography and tides as well as wind. Heavy rainfall can create freshwater flooding far from the coastline, including after the strongest winds have passed.

The National Hurricane Center issues multiple types of forecasts and warnings because no single number conveys all hazards. Emergency decisions should follow local forecasts and official instructions.

A good storm report explains the wind category without using it as a synonym for total danger. People experience hazards where they live, not on a scale printed in a headline.
