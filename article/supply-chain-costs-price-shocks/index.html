A shipping delay can cost more than the price of transportation

Global supply pressure affects inventories, production schedules and uncertainty. The path from disruption to consumer price is complicated.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessWhere Delays SpreadC·

When a shipment arrives late, the obvious cost is the delay. For a company that needs a component to finish production, the consequences can be much larger: idle equipment, missing sales or expensive last-minute alternatives.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index combines transportation prices and manufacturing indicators to gauge broad pressure in international supply networks. It is designed to capture more than one port or freight rate.

A supply chain includes suppliers, factories, logistics providers and inventories. Bottlenecks in one part can ripple outward, but the effect depends on contracts, substitutions and how much stock businesses already hold.

Prices also react to demand. A shipping problem during weak demand may have a different effect from the same problem when customers are competing for scarce goods. Blaming any one retail increase entirely on freight can be misleading.

Businesses can reduce some exposure through more flexible sourcing or inventory planning, but those choices bring their own costs. Keeping extra stock ties up money; relying on one supplier can leave a business vulnerable.

To read a supply-chain headline, ask which materials are affected, how long the disruption has lasted and whether evidence shows a broader market impact. A delayed container is a fact; its effect on inflation is an additional question.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed the New York Fed's supply-chain indicators and UNCTAD maritime-transport research. No individual shipment data were analyzed.
