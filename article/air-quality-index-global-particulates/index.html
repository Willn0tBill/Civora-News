Air quality can look clear and still be unhealthy.

Fine particles, ground-level ozone and inconsistent monitoring complicate how people interpret pollution levels across countries and cities.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
WorldThe Air You Cannot SeeC·

A skyline without visible smoke is not proof that the air is safe. Some pollutants are gases, and fine particulate matter can be too small to see individually. Air-quality reporting exists precisely because eyesight is a poor measuring instrument.

The World Health Organization identifies particulate matter, including PM2.5, as an important indicator of health risks associated with polluted air. The label refers to particles with aerodynamic diameters of 2.5 micrometers or less. Sources can include combustion, transport and other activities, while airborne particles can travel beyond the place that produced them.

The WHO's outdoor-air-pollution fact sheet estimates 4.2 million premature deaths globally in 2019 were attributable to ambient air pollution. That is a model-based health burden estimate for a specific year, not a count of every death certificate explicitly listing pollution.

In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency's Air Quality Index translates measured pollutant concentrations into categories of health concern. The index runs from 0 to 500, with higher values indicating greater pollution and concern. It can reflect ozone, particle pollution and other regulated pollutants depending on the reporting context.

An AQI value and a PM2.5 concentration are not interchangeable units. The former is an index built from pollutant-specific breakpoints, while the latter describes a physical concentration commonly reported in micrograms per cubic meter. A graphic that treats the two numbers as equivalent can be misleading.

International comparisons add another complication: monitoring networks, coverage and reporting methods differ. A city with few sensors might appear to have fewer pollution problems simply because less information is collected. Regional smoke, dust and industrial emissions also vary by season.

The clearest question is not just 'what color is the air-quality map?' It is which pollutant was measured, where the readings came from, how recently they were collected and what uncertainty remains. Pollution can cross borders; careful interpretation has to cross them too.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed WHO public-health data and U.S. EPA guidance. We did not measure air pollution in any city or calculate new health-burden estimates.
