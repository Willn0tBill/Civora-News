Earthquake magnitude tells you the size—not what every neighborhood felt

Magnitude describes the quake at its source. Shaking intensity varies by distance, ground conditions and built environment.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceMeasuring the ShakeC·

When an earthquake makes the news, the magnitude often leads the headline. It is a useful number, but it does not tell you exactly how strongly every building shook.

The U.S. Geological Survey distinguishes magnitude, which describes the size of the earthquake source, from intensity, which describes shaking at a particular location. One earthquake has a magnitude estimate, while different places can experience very different intensities.

Distance from the fault matters, but so can the local soil, earthquake depth and building characteristics. Two neighborhoods at similar distances may not experience identical effects.

Early magnitude estimates can be refined as more measurements are processed. A revised number does not necessarily indicate that the event itself changed or that the original report was dishonest.

Damage reports add another layer. The severity of structural damage depends on exposure and resilience as well as shaking. Using the word 'devastating' solely because of a magnitude number skips much of the real-world context.

A stronger earthquake headline includes where the quake occurred, which locations experienced intense shaking and which damage reports are verified. Magnitude begins the story; it does not finish it.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed USGS magnitude and intensity guidance. We did not analyze any new earthquake recordings.
