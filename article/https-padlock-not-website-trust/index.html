The browser padlock protects a connection, not the character of a website.

HTTPS helps keep traffic encrypted and authenticated in transit; a fraudulent site can also use HTTPS.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
TechnologyWhat the Lock Really SaysC·

A shopping site has a padlock beside its address, so it must be legitimate—right? Not necessarily. HTTPS is an important security technology, but it answers a narrower question than whether a merchant, news article or payment request deserves trust.

The Transport Layer Security system helps browsers establish encrypted connections and authenticate the server for a domain using certificates. It reduces opportunities for others on the network to read or alter information passing between browser and site.

That protection does not prove the person who registered the domain is honest. A convincing counterfeit login page can obtain its own valid certificate. The technical connection may be secure even while the site's purpose is deceptive.

The address bar therefore still matters. A familiar logo on an unfamiliar domain is not equivalent to the official website. Browser warnings, unexpected redirects and requests for sensitive information deserve attention independent of whether a lock appears.

HTTPS also does not evaluate the accuracy of the words on a page. A misleading article can be delivered over excellent encryption. Transport security and editorial integrity are different layers.

Readers should recognize the padlock as a baseline protection, not an endorsement. Ask both whether the connection is protected and whether the destination is the organization you intended to reach.
