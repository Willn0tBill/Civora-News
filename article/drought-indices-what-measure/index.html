A drought map is a judgment built from several measurements.

Drought monitors combine evidence about precipitation, soil, streams and regional conditions; one dry week does not tell the whole story.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
ScienceReading the Dry MapC·

A county turns orange on a drought map. The color is important, but it does not come from one universal instrument that directly measures 'drought' like a thermometer measures temperature.

The U.S. Drought Monitor uses multiple types of evidence and expert interpretation to classify conditions. Precipitation deficits, soil moisture, streamflow and other indicators help describe how prolonged dryness affects different places.

Drought itself takes several forms. Agricultural drought may show up in crop and soil conditions, while hydrological drought involves rivers, reservoirs and groundwater. A location can experience temporary rain without fully resolving a long-term water deficit.

National maps use consistent categories, but effects differ by region and season. A lack of rain that is ordinary in one climate may be exceptional in another. Comparing categories without local context can exaggerate or minimize impacts.

Map categories also are not forecasts guaranteeing what comes next. Conditions can improve or deteriorate, and weekly assessments should not be treated as a complete prediction.

Useful reporting identifies the map's date, indicators, geographic scale and actual impacts. Color is a summary of evidence, not the evidence itself.
