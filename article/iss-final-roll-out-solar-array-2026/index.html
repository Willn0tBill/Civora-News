The space station's final solar upgrade is installed. Here's why it matters.

A November 5 spacewalk finished a years-long power upgrade, raising the ISS's generating capacity from about 160 to 215 kilowatts.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Software & InfrastructureMore Power in OrbitC·

The International Space Station has been in orbit for decades. Keeping its systems working requires more than new experiments and routine maintenance. It also requires enough electricity to run an aging, constantly used laboratory hundreds of miles above Earth.

On November 5, 2026, NASA astronaut Anil Menon and Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk finished installing the eighth and final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array. NASA says the completed set raises the station's power-generating capacity from about 160 kilowatts to approximately 215 kilowatts.

The final spacewalk lasted six hours and 59 minutes, ending at 3:35 p.m. Eastern. The astronauts installed and deployed the array on the station's 3B power channel, completing a larger effort that began in 2021.

These solar arrays roll out rather than unfolding like some older rigid-panel designs. The approach adds usable generating capacity while working alongside existing station hardware. More available power supports station systems, scientific work and operations that depend on reliable electrical supply.

NASA says 18 spacewalks were involved in preparing equipment and installing the eight new arrays. The November 5 excursion was the 286th spacewalk supporting assembly, maintenance and upgrades of the orbiting station. Those numbers underline how much sustained work goes into an improvement that can look simple in a finished photograph.

The upgrade also has a less obvious purpose. NASA has said the additional electrical capacity supports critical station operations, including the eventual safe and controlled deorbit of the ISS. A power project completed in 2026 is therefore connected to decisions about the station's final phase as well as its present research.

The new figure—about 215 kilowatts—is generating capacity, not a guarantee that every device always consumes or receives that amount of electricity. Real operating conditions depend on sunlight, electrical routing, system health and demand.

The takeaway is straightforward: this was a practical infrastructure upgrade, not a new scientific discovery. It gives the station more electrical capability, but it does not eliminate every maintenance challenge facing a long-serving spacecraft.
