How NASA's Webb telescope studies worlds it cannot visit

Astronomers use light passing through distant atmospheres to infer what gases may be present, while carefully checking the limits of the evidence.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
ScienceReading StarlightC·

A planet around another star may be visible only through a tiny change in the star's light. Yet researchers can sometimes learn about its atmosphere without sending a spacecraft there. One method is transmission spectroscopy, which examines what happens to starlight as it passes through an atmosphere.

When a planet crosses its star from our viewpoint, some starlight travels through the planet's atmospheric gases before reaching an observatory. Molecules absorb light at characteristic wavelengths. Scientists examine the resulting spectrum and compare patterns with laboratory measurements and physical models.

NASA demonstrates this technique using a simulated spectrum of an Earth-like planet. Absorption features are not direct photographs of clouds, cities or oceans. They are measured differences in light that may support an inference about which molecules are present.

The James Webb Space Telescope is particularly sensitive to infrared wavelengths that contain useful information about certain atmospheric compounds. NASA explains how observations at different wavelengths help astronomers investigate the chemical composition of exoplanets.

But detection is not a simple matter of matching one dip in a chart. Observations contain noise, the host star may vary, and different atmospheric scenarios can sometimes produce similar spectral patterns. A proposed explanation needs uncertainty estimates and tests against alternatives.

Even a molecule associated with life on Earth would not be an automatic discovery of life elsewhere. Chemistry can arise through multiple processes, and a potential signal must be considered alongside the planet's temperature, environment and other evidence.

The impressive part of this research is not only the distance involved. It is the ability to extract information from extraordinarily faint light while distinguishing what the instrument measured from what researchers infer. Responsible science reporting preserves that distinction.

Reporting note: This Civora explainer draws on NASA's publicly available descriptions of transmission spectroscopy and does not announce a new planetary discovery.
