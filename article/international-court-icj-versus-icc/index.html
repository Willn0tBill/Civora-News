Diplomacy & International Law / EXPLAINER

The world's two famous international courts do very different jobs.

The ICJ resolves certain disputes between states; the ICC prosecutes individuals accused of specified international crimes.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Diplomacy & International LawTwo Courts, Two MandatesC·

A court in The Hague makes an announcement and social media declares that 'the international court' has convicted an entire country. That language is often wrong before the argument even begins. Two major international courts operate under different legal mandates.

The International Court of Justice, the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, addresses legal disputes submitted by states and issues advisory opinions when authorized international bodies request them. Its jurisdiction and the effect of its orders depend on the legal basis of the proceeding.

The International Criminal Court is a separate institution established under the Rome Statute. Its cases concern the criminal responsibility of individuals for crimes within its jurisdiction, subject to important limits and procedural requirements.

An ICJ provisional measure is not a final judgment on every allegation, just as an ICC arrest warrant is not a conviction. Evidence, procedure and the rights of parties or defendants matter at every stage.

Not all countries have accepted the same jurisdictional arrangements. The legal scope of a particular proceeding must be checked before assuming a court can hear it or enforce a result against any person or state.

Precise reporting identifies the court by name, the parties or accused persons, the procedural stage and what the order actually says. The authority of international law is weakened when readers are told that one court did the work of another.
