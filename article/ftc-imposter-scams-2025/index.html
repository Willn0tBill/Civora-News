Scammers impersonated trusted names. The reported damage: $3.5 billion.

The FTC says people reported losing $3.5 billion to imposter scams in 2025. Fake bank warnings and government messages turn ordinary trust into a weapon.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
BusinessThe Cost of DeceptionC·

A message says your bank account is in danger. Another claims to be from a government office. Both may look official, and both can be part of the same business model: making someone panic before they stop to check who is contacting them.

According to data the Federal Trade Commission released in June 2026, consumers reported losing $3.5 billion to imposter scams in 2025. Imposter scams made up nearly one in three fraud reports received by the agency. Those figures describe reported incidents, not every case of fraud that actually occurred.

The tactics do not depend on a single app or platform. The FTC describes impersonation through calls, text messages, email, social media and search results. The critical move is the claim of authority: a familiar company, agency or person supposedly needs you to act immediately.

Financial impersonation can be particularly costly. A scammer posing as a bank representative may insist that a customer move money to a supposedly safe account. The request sounds like protection, but it can put funds directly in the fraudster's control.

The FTC reported nearly $1 billion in losses to business impersonators and about $920 million to government impersonators in 2025. Bank impersonators accounted for the largest reported business-impersonation losses. Total reported losses across fraud categories reached roughly $16 billion.

These are serious numbers, but precision matters. 'Reported losses' does not mean the FTC audited every transaction or that these totals capture all victims. Some people never report fraud. The agency's dataset shows the scale of the reports it received and how that picture changed over time.

The practical defense begins with an independent check. An unexpected message demanding money, credentials or an urgent transfer should not be verified using the same message's link or phone number. Contact the organization through a channel you found yourself.

Impersonation succeeds because it exploits trust, not because a message is necessarily sophisticated technology. The strongest warning sign is not always a typo. Sometimes it is a convincing voice telling you there is no time to verify.

Reporting note: This is Civora's analysis of FTC consumer-report data, not an investigation of particular victims or an original interview.
