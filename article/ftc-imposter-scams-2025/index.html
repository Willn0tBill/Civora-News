Imposter scams drew $3.5 billion in reported losses in 2025, FTC says

Federal figures show how fake bank alerts and other impersonation schemes contributed to billions in reported fraud losses.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-09
BusinessThe Cost of DeceptionC·

An urgent message that appears to come from a bank or government office may be the opening move in a costly deception. The Federal Trade Commission reported in June 2026 that people said they lost $3.5 billion to imposter scams during 2025, with scammers borrowing the identities of recognizable organizations and trusted contacts.

Imposter schemes accounted for nearly one in three fraud reports received by the FTC that year. The agency said people encountered them through calls, texts, email, social media, search results and other channels. The formats change, but the common tactic is to claim authority that has not actually been established.

Some of the costliest reported schemes begin with a fake security warning. Someone posing as a financial institution claims an account is at risk and tells a person to move money to keep it safe. The supposed emergency makes an unusual request sound like necessary protection.

The FTC said people reported losing nearly one billion dollars to business impersonators, with bank impersonators responsible for the highest reported business-impersonation losses. Government impersonators accounted for about $920 million. Both figures were higher than their counterparts in 2024.

Across all fraud categories, reported losses reached about $16 billion in 2025, approximately 25 percent above 2024, according to the release. The word reported is critical: these totals describe information consumers submitted to the agency, not a complete accounting of every fraudulent incident or dollar lost.

The distinction between a message and verification can help people recognize suspicious demands. An unexpected request for credentials, payment or a transfer should be checked through an independently located contact channel. A link, phone number or caller ID supplied by the alarming message is not, by itself, confirmation of authenticity.

The findings show why deception can be powerful even without a technical breach. Scammers may cause significant harm by persuading people to act on fabricated authority. Public fraud data can describe the scale of reported losses; individual situations still require careful verification.

Reporting note: This article is Civora's source-based analysis of the FTC's published consumer-report figures, not an interview-based investigation.
