A public-records request asks for documents, not answers on demand.

FOIA can open federal records to scrutiny, but it has jurisdictional limits, exemptions and no requirement that agencies invent an analysis.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
U.S.The Public RecordC·

An agency says it made a decision based on evidence. You want to see the evidence. The Freedom of Information Act, or FOIA, is one route into federal records—but it is not a universal button that forces every public official to answer every question.

FOIA.gov explains that requests should describe existing agency records and go to the office likely to hold them. There is no single government office that processes every request, and requesters do not need a special form. Checking an agency's existing public document library can avoid asking for information already online.

A critical distinction is the difference between records and research. The Department of Justice says FOIA generally does not require an agency to conduct new analysis, answer questionnaires or create a document just because someone wants one. Asking for a particular contract or set of emails is different from asking the agency to write a report explaining whether the contract was worthwhile.

FOIA principally applies to federal executive-branch agencies. It does not provide the same route into Congress, federal court records, or state and local governments. Those institutions have separate procedures and, in the case of states, their own public-records statutes.

Access is not unlimited. Federal law includes exemptions that can protect personal privacy, national security and certain law-enforcement interests. An exemption is a legal reason to withhold qualifying information; it should not be treated as automatic proof that wrongdoing occurred.

Agencies may acknowledge a request with a tracking number, process records and withhold or redact parts of them. Requesters can use applicable administrative appeal procedures to challenge decisions. Processing time varies, particularly for complex or large requests.

The practical reporting lesson is to narrow the question before filing. Identify which agency has the record, what it might be called, and what date range matters. An incomplete response is not necessarily the end of an investigation, but neither is the filing itself evidence that the requested material exists.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed FOIA.gov and Department of Justice explanatory material. We did not submit a FOIA request for this article or claim access to unreleased records.
