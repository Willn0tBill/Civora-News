A library may buy an e-book but not own it like a printed book.

Digital lending commonly involves license terms that differ from physical ownership, affecting access, cost and collection planning.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
Media & PublishingThe Book You Can't KeepC·

A library pays for an electronic book and then loses access when a license expires. That outcome seems strange beside a printed book that can stay on a shelf for decades, but digital lending often operates under different contractual terms.

The American Library Association describes how licensing models can limit e-book access by time, loan count or simultaneous users. The public may experience the same reading experience, while the library manages a very different purchase.

Printed copies are physical property subject to established rules governing lending. An e-book file can be controlled by a contract with technical restrictions, even when the library pays a substantial amount.

Licensing can affect library budgets and patron waitlists. A popular title may require repeated payments to preserve availability, while other books may not be offered under the same lending terms.

Digital materials also introduce preservation and accessibility questions. The ability to read a file today does not guarantee compatible technology and permission to make it available in the future.

A clear article identifies whether an institution bought a physical copy, licensed digital access or subscribed to a collection. The word 'purchased' is too vague when the rights differ.
