October prices rose 0.4%. A missing comparison makes the story more complicated.

The latest consumer price report shows renewed pressure from food, shelter and energy, but missing October 2025 survey data limit some year-over-year comparisons.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
BusinessThe October Price PuzzleC·

Prices increased again in October, and the details make the headline more revealing than the headline number alone. The Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers rose 0.4% on a seasonally adjusted basis, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics release on November 10, 2026. That followed a 0.6% increase in September. Slower than the previous month is not the same thing as falling prices.

Food prices rose 0.5% in October. The index for groceries increased 0.4%, while eating away from home cost 0.5% more on the agency's measure. Those categories matter because an aggregate index can look modest while recurring purchases still feel expensive to households with different spending habits.

Shelter costs climbed 0.3% and accounted for more than 30% of the monthly increase in the overall index. Energy prices increased 0.7%, following a much larger 5.2% September rise. The BLS also reported a 0.3% monthly increase in its measure that excludes food and energy.

One of the most important details is a missing comparison. The BLS said it did not collect October 2025 survey data because of a lapse in appropriations. For most consumer price series, the agency therefore could not publish the usual October-to-October 12-month percentage changes for 2026. A chart that quietly fills in those missing figures as though they were observed would be misleading.

Monthly and annual inflation answer different questions. A seasonally adjusted monthly rate describes movement from September to October; a 12-month rate describes change across a longer interval using a separate comparison. Neither tells you directly how much an individual shopper paid or how their wages changed.

Some categories moved in opposite directions. The report identified decreases in used cars and trucks and in communication costs among major indexes, even as food, shelter and medical care contributed upward pressure. A national average is not a price tag on every product or neighborhood.

For readers following interest rates, this report is one input—not an automatic command to the Federal Reserve. Policymakers also consider labor markets, broader inflation trends and uncertainty. The disciplined reading is straightforward: October's price index rose, the pace eased compared with September, and an important piece of the usual annual comparison is unavailable.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed the BLS October 2026 CPI news release and the agency's explanation of missing historical data. This is an analysis of published statistics, not an independent price survey or interviews with shoppers.
