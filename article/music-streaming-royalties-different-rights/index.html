A music stream can generate different royalties for different people

Songwriters, performers and recording owners may receive different payments under different licensing systems. One 'per-stream rate' rarely tells the whole story.

BY Civora Research DeskPUBLISHED 2026-11-10
CultureBehind the StreamC·

A song plays on a streaming service, and someone asks how much the artist earned from that one play. The question sounds simple until the rights behind the recording are separated. A musical work and a sound recording can have different owners and different royalty arrangements.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that the Music Modernization Act established an important licensing system for certain interactive digital uses of musical compositions. The Mechanical Licensing Collective administers specific mechanical royalties under that framework.

SoundExchange handles a different category: statutory digital performance royalties for certain non-interactive uses of sound recordings. It distributes payments to eligible performers and recording-rights holders under the applicable system.

Those arrangements do not mean every platform pays the same amount or that every person associated with a song receives money from the same organization. Contracts, territories, services and rights can alter the result.

Publishing statements about a universal payment per stream without specifying the service and right being measured can mislead independent musicians. A dollar figure divorced from those details tells a partial story at best.

The practical starting point for creators is to identify the songwriters, publishers, performers and recording owners, then understand which licenses and collection systems apply to the uses of their work. Clear metadata and agreements matter.

Reporting note: Civora reviewed the Copyright Office's Music Modernization Act materials and SoundExchange explanations. This is general information, not individual royalty advice.
